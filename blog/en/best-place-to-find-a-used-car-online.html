Best place to find a used car online: sources and how to compare

No single site is the best place to find a used car online. You find cars faster if you write down your criteria first, check several sources of listings, set up alerts for new ads and compare every offer in one log. This guide is for private buyers looking for a car at home or abroad. As of October 2026.

What to write down before you open the first site

A search goes faster when you already know what you will turn down. Note:

  • your total budget, which includes transport, registration, any taxes and a first service on top of the price;
  • the model and generation, not only the make, because engines and known faults differ between generations;
  • engine, gearbox and fuel;
  • a ceiling for mileage and age;
  • equipment you will not do without, such as a tow bar or a reversing camera;
  • the longest distance you will travel to see a car;
  • reasons for an immediate no, such as no service history or a seller who will not give the chassis number.

Why one site is not enough

Each site holds its own set of listings, so one car can appear in several places while another appears in only one. If you search on a single site, you see part of the market.

You have three ways to widen it. The first is manual searches on a few sources with the same filters. The second is a saved search with an alert for new ads, which many sites offer, so check whether yours does. The third is a search engine that combines several portals. The same rule applies to all three: a well-priced car often sells quickly, so how fast you notice a new ad matters.

A search log: a table for comparing offers

A spreadsheet log keeps offers from blurring together and, after a few days, shows you a realistic price for your criteria. Each offer gets one row.

ColumnWhat to enterWhy

Chassis number (VIN)Yes or no, and when you received itA seller who will not give it is already a warning

Once you have several entries for the same model, you can see which offers fall outside the normal range. Until you know why an offer is far cheaper than the rest, do not trust it.

Red flags in a listing

A suspicious listing is usually given away by a combination of signs. Be careful if:

  • the price is far below comparable offers and there is no explanation;
  • the seller does not answer questions about the chassis number or service history;
  • they ask for payment in advance or suggest an "agent" who will hold the money safely;
  • the photos look like they were taken in different places, or the description is thin or copied;
  • they rush you because "many other people are interested".

Checks before you pay

Do the checks in this order, because the early ones cost least and weed out bad offers fastest.

  1. Get the chassis number and photos of the vehicle documents.
  2. Check the vehicle history with an independent, usually paid, service rather than relying only on the seller.
  3. View the car yourself, or have an independent expert inspect it if it is far away.
  4. Sign a sales contract that names the vehicle and the seller.
  5. Take the original documents: registration certificate, service book and invoice.
  6. Pay on collection or through a secure channel, never in advance without safeguards.

Frequently asked questions

How many sites should I check?

Enough to cover at least one large local source and, if you are open to buying abroad, one European one. Every extra source adds work, so add one only when your log shows the local ones are not enough.

Is it worth searching abroad?

Only if the price gap survives all the costs: transport, registration and any taxes. Check the official sources first, and only then think about viewing a car.

How do I know a price is realistic?

Compare it in your log with several ads for the same model, year and similar mileage. An offer that stands out on the low side needs an explanation before you take it seriously.
