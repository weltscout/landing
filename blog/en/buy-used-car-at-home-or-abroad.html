Used cars: is it worth buying in Slovenia or in another EU country?

Buying abroad pays off only when the price gap is larger than every extra cost and risk, and there is no general answer. Besides the price, you weigh VAT, registration tax, transport, plates, paperwork and legal protection, which is very limited if you buy from a private seller abroad. This guide is for private buyers and dealers in Slovenia and any other EU country and gives no invented prices. As of October 2026.

When does buying a used car abroad pay off?

When the price abroad is lower by more than all the extra costs and a reserve for faults. The European Consumer Centres advise comparing prices and every additional cost, not just the purchase price, because standard equipment and technical features differ between countries (ECC-Net, Your Europe). A simple formula helps:

net saving = price difference − (transport + plates and insurance + taxes and fees + inspections + travel + reserve for faults)

If the result is close to zero, buying at home is usually the more sensible choice, because viewing, test driving and complaints are easier.

The formula above applies in Slovenia too. What changes are a few fixed steps that the Slovenian rules add when the car comes from another EU country.

What is your legal protection from a dealer and from a private seller?

EU consumer rules protect you when a business sells you a car, but not when a private person does (Your Europe).

SellerWhat applies
Dealer in the EUA legal guarantee of at least two years. In some countries it can be reduced to no less than one year for a used car if both sides agree at purchase. A defect that appears in the first year is presumed to have existed at delivery.
Private personConsumer law does not apply. Your rights are extremely limited, and a civil action can be costly and difficult.

According to Your Europe you should tell the seller about a defect as soon as possible and not later than two months after you find it, though the details can differ by country. If a trader in another EU country will not help, the European Consumer Centre in your own country can.

Checklist before you decide

  1. Compare the same equipment and model year, not only the price.
  2. Add every extra cost using the formula above, then add a reserve for repairs.
  3. Check the seller in public company registers and confirm the contact details (ECC-Net).
  4. Ask for the vehicle's serial number, proof of ownership, service history and registration papers, and check that they are genuine (ECC-Net).
  5. Pay by a secure method. Avoid advance deposits to unknown accounts (ECC-Net).
  6. Do not plan to drive home on the plates of your old car. Arrange temporary plates and insurance that are valid in every country on the route (Your Europe).
  7. Read the contract before you sign, especially the guarantee period and any reduction for a used car.

What this means for a private buyer and for a dealer

For a private buyer

If you want a car for yourself and worry more about risk than saving, a dealer at home is usually the simplest route. Searching abroad makes most sense when the model you want is rare locally. Whichever way you go, prefer a dealer to a private seller, because the legal guarantee applies.

A dealer judges each car by its net saving: purchase price abroad, import and registration costs, and the margin after resale. Frequently asked questions

Is it cheaper to buy a car abroad?

There is no general answer. We have no verified, dated source for an average price gap between countries, so we do not give one. Compare the same model, equipment and year, and add every extra cost.

Can I buy a car in another country and drive it home?

Yes, as long as you have temporary plates from the country of purchase and insurance valid on your route. You cannot use the plates of your old car, and temporary plates are not accepted everywhere in the EU (Your Europe).

Which country is the cheapest for a used car?

We cannot name one. Prices depend on the model, the age and the seller, and the sources we use give no comparison between countries.

Do I pay VAT twice?

You should not. A used car carries the VAT shown on the dealer's invoice and none at home. A car that is new for VAT carries VAT in the country where you register it, and you can claim back any VAT paid abroad (Your Europe).
